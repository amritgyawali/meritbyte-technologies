The best website developer in Nepal is not the one at the top of a "top 10" list; it is the one that passes checks you can run yourself in an hour or two. They show live sites that load quickly on an ordinary Android phone, put your domain, hosting and code in your name, give a written scope with payments tied to milestones, and have already built what you need locally, such as an eSewa checkout or a Nepali Unicode page.

The 15 checks below apply equally to a freelancer, a small studio and a large web development company in Nepal. Run them across your shortlist and the right choice is usually obvious.

## What makes the best website developer in Nepal for your project?

Fit, evidence and ownership, in that order. The right developer has built projects like yours, can prove it with live work, and leaves you owning everything at the end. Size, awards and ranking positions say little about any of those.

Ranking lists of the "top IT company in Nepal" are a starting point for names, not a verdict. Many are paid placements, some are published by one of the listed companies, and almost none test what decides whether your project goes well: who will actually work on it, what you will own, and what happens when something breaks after launch.

Size is a weak signal too. A large software company may be excellent at banking systems and a poor fit for a 10-page hotel site; a two-person studio may be exactly the right size for it. Treat every company, including ours, as a set of claims to verify. ## Proof of work: checks 1 to 4

### 1. Live sites you can open on your own phone

Ask for five live URLs, not screenshots or design mockups. Open them on your phone using mobile data. Tap through the menus, submit a contact form, and check that each site still carries the client's own contact details. Mockups show that someone can design; live sites show they can ship.

### 2. Speed you measure, not speed they claim

Run three of those sites through Google's PageSpeed Insights. Where field data exists, compare it with Google's "good" Core Web Vitals thresholds: LCP within 2.5 seconds, INP within 200 milliseconds and CLS within 0.1. A developer whose client sites fail badly on mobile will likely build yours the same way. Our [Core Web Vitals explainer](/blog/core-web-vitals-explained) covers what the numbers mean.

### 3. Their own website and presence

Their site should be fast, current and clear about who they are and how to reach them. A Google Business Profile with genuine reviews, an address that matches their registration documents and a phone number someone answers are basic signs of a business that expects to be around next year.

### 4. Two clients you can phone

Ask for two clients from the last two years whose sites you have seen, then call them. Three questions are enough: Did they launch when promised? What happened when something went wrong? Would you hire them again? Five minutes on the phone is worth more than any testimonial page.

## Ownership and paperwork: checks 5 to 8

### 5. A registered business that issues a proper bill

A company should be able to show its registration certificate from the Office of the Company Registrar and a PAN or VAT certificate from the Inland Revenue Department. If your business claims input VAT, you need a VAT bill for the 13% VAT. Freelancers suit many projects, but know which one you are hiring.

### 6. Your name on the domain, hosting and code

Ask directly: "Whose name and email will the domain and hosting be registered under?" The answer should be your business, with you holding the login. The same goes for the code repository, Google Analytics, Search Console and your Google Business Profile. Developers who keep these "for convenience" make it hard for you to leave, whether they intend to or not.

### 7. A written scope and payments tied to milestones

A good proposal lists pages or templates, features, who supplies content, revision rounds and a timeline, then links payments to delivered milestones. A deposit upfront is normal; the full amount upfront is not. Our guide to [comparing web development quotes](/blog/how-to-compare-web-development-quotes) shows how to line proposals up side by side.

### 8. Licensed themes and plugins

Ask whether premium themes and plugins will be licensed in your name. "Nulled" copies, pirated versions of paid WordPress products, are common in cheap builds. They cannot be updated safely and are a frequent route for malware. A licence costs a few thousand rupees a year; cleaning up a hacked site costs far more.

## Local know-how: checks 9 to 11

### 9. Payment integrations they have actually built

If you will sell online, ask to see a live checkout they built with eSewa, Khalti, Fonepay or ConnectIPS, and ask how the site confirms a payment. The right answer involves checking the transaction with the provider's server before marking an order paid, not trusting the browser redirect. Our [eSewa, Khalti and Fonepay integration guide](/blog/esewa-khalti-fonepay-integration) explains why this matters.

### 10. Nepali Unicode, not Preeti

If any of your site will be in Nepali, ask to see a Nepali page they built and paste some of its text into a search box. If it comes out as Latin gibberish, it was typed in a legacy font such as Preeti, and search engines cannot read it. See [building a Nepali-English bilingual website](/blog/nepali-english-bilingual-website) for how it should be done.

### 11. SEO basics included, not sold separately

Page titles, meta descriptions, an XML sitemap, Google Search Console, sensible URLs and 301 redirects from any old site belong in the build, not in a later "SEO package". Be wary of anyone promising first-page positions on a timetable; Google's own guidance warns against firms that do.

## After launch: checks 12 to 15

### 12. Progress you can see during the build

Ask how you will see the work before launch. A staging URL you can open any time, one named person who answers your questions, and a written update each week make problems visible while they are still cheap to fix.

### 13. Security, backups and monitoring

Ask where backups are stored (off the server, and for how many days), who applies updates, whether HTTPS covers every page, and who is alerted when the site goes down. "The hosting company does backups" is not a complete answer.

### 14. A proper handover

At the end you should receive every login, a short document explaining how the site is built and hosted, and training for whoever will edit content. Ask to see a handover document from a past project, with client details removed.

### 15. Support terms in writing

Ask what is included after launch: the length of the bug-fix period, the monthly maintenance price, what it covers and the response time. "We will always support you" is kind but impossible to hold anyone to.

## Red flags that should end the conversation

- Full payment upfront, or no written agreement at all.
- "We will register the domain for you" under their own account.
- Promises of first-page positions on Google by a fixed date.
- No live sites to show, only designs or templates.
- Reluctance to name who will build your site.
- Pressure to decide today.

## Questions to ask on the first call

1. Who exactly will work on my project, and what else are they working on?
2. What do you need from me, and by when?
3. What happens if the project runs late because of you, or because of me?
4. What will I own at the end, and where is it registered?
5. What will it cost to keep the site running for a year after launch?

Price matters too, but compare it only after these answers. Typical ranges are in our guide to [how much a website costs in Nepal](/blog/website-cost-in-nepal).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it better to hire a web development company in Nepal or a freelancer?

It depends on the project. A capable freelancer suits a small, well-defined site where you supply the content. A company suits projects with payments, integrations, several stakeholders or long-term maintenance, because it can cover holidays, illness and staff changes. Whichever you choose, apply the same checks: live work, ownership in your name, a written scope and written support terms.

### How can I verify that a web company in Nepal is registered?

Ask for the company registration certificate and the PAN or VAT certificate, and check that the names match the company on your contract and invoices. The Inland Revenue Department's website offers a lookup by PAN. A registered company should issue a proper bill for every payment, and a VAT-registered one should issue a VAT bill.

### How much should I pay upfront for a website in Nepal?

A deposit is normal, commonly somewhere between a quarter and a half of the first milestone, with the rest tied to agreed deliverables such as design approval and launch. Paying everything before any work is shown leaves you with nothing to hold back if the project stalls. Whatever split you agree, write it into the contract.

### Does my website developer need to be in Kathmandu?

No. Most of a website project runs over calls, shared documents and a staging site, so a good developer in Pokhara, Butwal or anywhere else can serve a Kathmandu business well. Being nearby helps with photo shoots and in-person training, but it matters far less than live work, ownership terms and a clear written scope.
