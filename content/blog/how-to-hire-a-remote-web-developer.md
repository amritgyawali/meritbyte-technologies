The safe way to hire a remote web developer is to make each step prove something. A written brief proves you know what you want; live work and references prove they have done it before; a small paid trial proves they can do it for you. A contract and your own accounts then make sure that if it still goes wrong, you keep everything. Most hiring horror stories skip at least one of those steps.

## What should you prepare before you hire a remote web developer?

Prepare a one-page brief and your own accounts. Both take an afternoon and change the quality of every conversation that follows.

The brief should cover the goal of the site or feature, who it is for, the pages or functions that must exist, integrations (payments, CRM, booking), the deadline, a budget range and who approves the work. Our [website project brief template](/blog/website-project-brief-template) gives you the headings.

Then decide three things:

- **The model.** A freelancer, an agency, a dedicated developer from a firm, or a full-time remote employee hired through an employer of record such as Deel or Remote. Our comparison of [freelancers and agencies](/blog/freelancer-vs-web-development-agency) helps with the first choice.
- **The overlap you need.** Two hours a day of shared working time is enough for most web projects; some need more.
- **The accounts you will own.** A GitHub or GitLab organization, the hosting account, the domain registrar and a shared vault in a password manager such as 1Password or Bitwarden. Create them before anyone starts, so nothing is ever set up in someone else's name.

## Where should you look for a remote web developer?

Start with referrals, then use platforms to widen the shortlist. Every source works; each has a characteristic way of going wrong.

| Source | Good for | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Referrals from businesses like yours | The strongest signal you will get | A friend's developer who has never built what you need |
| Freelance platforms such as Upwork or Toptal | Fast shortlists; milestone escrow on some platforms | Reviews that can be gamed; the person on the call not being the person doing the work |
| Agency directories such as Clutch | Firms with verified-looking reviews | Sponsored placements; check reviews by contacting clients yourself |
| LinkedIn and GitHub | Real work history and real code | Slow, and strong engineers are not always strong communicators |

Aim for three to five candidates. More than that and you will not vet any of them properly.

## How do you vet a remote web developer's past work?

Test their live work yourself and speak to people who paid for it. Screenshots and portfolio pages prove only that someone can use a screenshot tool.

1. **Open three live sites they built.** Run each through Google's PageSpeed Insights on mobile, fill in a form, and try moving through a page using only the Tab key. You will learn more in ten minutes than from any pitch.
2. **Ask exactly what they did on each.** Design, build, both, or one part of a larger team? Vague answers are an answer.
3. **Ask for a code walkthrough.** A screen share of a repository they are allowed to show, explaining why it is structured the way it is.
4. **Call two past clients.** Ask whether dates were hit, what went wrong and how it was handled, who owns the accounts now, and whether they would hire the developer again.
5. **Verify identity.** Hold interviews on video, and make sure the person you interview is the person who will work on your project. US authorities, including the FBI, have repeatedly warned about fraudulent remote IT workers using stolen or fake identities.

## Remote developer interview questions that reveal how someone works

The most useful remote developer interview questions are about process and ownership, not frameworks. Technical skill shows in the trial; these questions show whether working together will be pleasant.

| Question | What a good answer sounds like |
| --- | --- |
| Tell me about a project that went wrong. What did you change afterwards? | A specific story, some of the blame taken, a concrete change in how they work |
| How would you split this project into milestones? | A small first milestone that can be demonstrated on a staging site |
| Where will the code, hosting and domain live? | "In your accounts. I'll need my own login." |
| What happens when I ask for something outside the scope? | They write it down, estimate it and ask before starting |
| What will I receive at the end of each week? | A written update, a staging link, what is next and what is blocked |
| How do you test before a release? | Named practices: staging, device and browser checks, automated tests where they pay off, an accessibility check |
| Which hours will overlap with mine, and how fast do you reply? | A specific window and a reply time they are willing to write into the contract |
| Who else will work on this? | Names, and an upfront mention of any subcontractors |
| What kind of project would you turn down? | An honest boundary, which suggests they will also be honest about problems |

## Why run a paid trial, and what should it be?

A paid trial is the only reliable test of how someone works with you. Keep it small, real and paid at their normal rate, and never ask for free "spec work".

Good trials take roughly four to ten hours: build one page template from your design, fix a known bug on your current site, or set up the repository, staging environment and deployment pipeline for the project. Watch how they behave as much as what they deliver. Did they ask sensible questions early? Did they finish within their own estimate, or explain why not before it ran over? Is the code in your repository, with a readable commit history?

## What should the contract and setup include?

The contract should make ownership and exit simple. If you hire an offshore developer in another jurisdiction, you will rarely go to court, so the protection has to come from the structure itself.

- **IP assignment** to you on payment, with a license for any pre-existing components they reuse.
- **Milestones with acceptance criteria**, and payment per milestone after you have checked the work on staging.
- **Your accounts**: code in your organization's repository, hosting and domain in your name, individual logins you can revoke, credentials shared only through the password manager.
- **Confidentiality**, plus data processing terms if they will touch personal data, which GDPR and UK GDPR require.
- **A bug-fix period** after launch, and a clear rate for work after that.
- **Termination terms**: notice, handover of all code and documentation, and access removal.

Pay through platform escrow, or directly by bank or services such as Wise or Payoneer. A modest deposit is normal; paying the full amount upfront is not.

## What are the red flags when you hire an offshore developer?

Most bad hires show warning signs in the first two weeks. Stop early if you see these:

- A request for full payment before any work is shown.
- The domain or hosting registered in their name "for convenience".
- Progress shown only in screenshots, with no staging site.
- Reluctance to join a video call, or a noticeably different person on later calls.
- No clear explanation of their own portfolio work.
- Promises of guaranteed Google rankings.
- A fixed quote given without a single question about your business.
- A request for your main admin password instead of their own account.
- Silences of several days during the first week.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does it cost to hire a remote web developer?

It depends mostly on where the developer is and how senior they are. As typical market ranges, US freelancers commonly charge about $50 to $150 an hour, while offshore agencies commonly charge about $25 to $75. Rates vary widely by specialism. Compare estimated hours for the whole job, not the hourly rate alone, because a slower developer at a lower rate can cost more.

### Should I hire through a freelance platform or directly?

Platforms help when you have no referrals: they offer quick shortlists, payment escrow and dispute processes, in exchange for fees. Hiring directly is cheaper over a long engagement and gives you a direct contract. Either way, the vetting is the same: live work, references, a video interview and a paid trial. Do not rely on platform ratings alone.

### How long should a paid trial project be?

Roughly four to ten hours of work is enough to see how someone communicates, estimates and writes code, without either side over-investing. Choose a real task you need anyway, such as one page template, a bug fix or setting up the project's repository and staging site. Pay their normal rate, and make sure the work lands in your own repository.

### How do I protect my code if a remote developer disappears?

Own the infrastructure from day one. Keep the repository in your organization, the hosting and domain in your accounts, and credentials in your password manager, with the developer using individual logins you can revoke. Require code to be pushed at least daily. Then losing a developer costs you time to find a replacement, not the project itself.
