A trekking agency website wins international bookings by answering, on the trip page itself, every question a traveller in Germany, the US or Australia would otherwise email about: altitude each night, walking hours, what the price includes, which permits are needed, group size, departure dates and the full cost. Then it makes holding a place with a deposit easy, and a person replies within hours rather than days.

Most Nepali trekking sites list the same Everest, Annapurna and Langtang itineraries at similar prices. Travellers choose on specifics and trust, and that is where the website does its work.

## Why do trekkers choose one agency over another?

Because one agency looked more specific, more accountable and quicker to respond. When ten itineraries look the same, travellers pick the company that makes them comfortable sending money to people they have never met, on the other side of the world.

Trust signals that matter to international trekkers:

- **Licence and memberships they can check.** Your Department of Tourism registration, TAAN (Trekking Agencies' Association of Nepal) membership and company registration number, shown with the numbers.
- **Named guides with photos**, the languages they speak, and training such as wilderness first aid.
- **Independent reviews** on Google and Tripadvisor, linked from the site rather than only pasted into it.
- **A safety section**: how you handle acclimatisation, what happens when someone shows signs of altitude sickness, and how evacuation is arranged.
- **A real person replying**, with a name, quickly.

## What should each trip page on a trekking agency website include?

Everything a careful traveller needs to decide and prepare, arranged so the key facts are visible without reading a long story first. Use this as the template:

1. **Summary box:** duration, trekking days, maximum altitude, difficulty, group size, start and end points, best seasons.
2. **Day-by-day itinerary:** start and end village, overnight altitude, walking hours and distance, type of accommodation.
3. **Acclimatisation days**, clearly marked, with the reason for each.
4. **Elevation profile or route map**, as a lightweight image.
5. **Price and inclusions:** permits, guide and porter wages and insurance, meals, lodging, domestic flights or road transport.
6. **Exclusions:** international flights, visa, travel insurance, tips, extra nights in Kathmandu.
7. **Permits required** for the route, with a "last checked" date.
8. **Fixed departure dates** with availability, and a private-trip option.
9. **Price by group size**, plus any single supplement.
10. **Fitness needed**, in plain terms ("comfortable walking six hours a day, several days in a row").
11. **Insurance requirement**, including helicopter evacuation cover at the route's altitudes.
12. **Packing list** for the season.
13. **Cancellation and refund policy**, with dates and percentages.
14. **FAQ** built from real enquiries about that trek.

Structured like this, the page also serves search engines and AI assistants, which lift facts such as maximum altitude and number of days straight from clearly labelled content.

## How should permit information be handled?

Accurately, route by route, with the date you last checked it. Requirements differ by region and change: TIMS cards, national park and conservation area permits (Sagarmatha National Park, the Annapurna Conservation Area, Langtang National Park), local area fees, and Restricted Area Permits for places such as Manaslu and Upper Mustang.

Rules on guides have changed too. Since 2023, foreign trekkers in many areas have been required to trek with a licensed guide arranged through a registered agency, with regional exceptions. Because rules and fees shift, do not print them on dozens of pages. Keep one maintained permit section per region, link each trip to it, and a single update fixes every page.

## How can international travellers pay a deposit?

By card, if at all possible, through a Nepali bank's online card payment service. Stripe does not onboard Nepal-registered businesses and PayPal does not offer Nepali accounts that receive payments, so your bank's card service and international bank transfer are the realistic routes.

A workable setup:

- **Card deposit online** through your bank's hosted payment page, with the amount and currency clear before paying.
- **Bank transfer (SWIFT)** as a fallback for large groups, with charges stated.
- **Balance paid on arrival** or before the trek starts, as your terms say.
- **Nepali wallets** such as eSewa and Khalti for domestic trekkers; see our [eSewa, Khalti and Fonepay integration guide](/blog/esewa-khalti-fonepay-integration).

Show prices in US dollars if that is how you quote, and say whether card fees are added. Put the refund policy next to the payment button, not only in the terms.

## Where are bookings actually won?

In the reply. An enquiry sent at 9 pm in London arrives at 1:45 am in Nepal in the British summer, or 2:45 am in winter, so the traveller's first impression is your autoresponder and how soon a person follows up.

What helps:

- An automatic reply that is genuinely useful: the trip's printable itinerary, the next departure dates and when a person will write back.
- A WhatsApp button, since many travellers prefer it, with conversations logged so nothing is lost when someone is off.
- A simple pipeline, in a CRM or even a shared board, tracking each enquiry from question to quote to deposit.
- Quotes personalised with the traveller's dates and questions, not pasted from a template.

Count how many enquiries become deposits for each trip. It tells you which pages and prices work better than traffic figures ever will.

## How can a Nepali trekking company outrank global operators?

By being more specific than they can be. Large international operators rank for "Everest Base Camp trek"; an agency with guides on the ground can win longer searches such as "Manaslu circuit in December", "Annapurna base camp with children" or "Langtang trek after the monsoon".

Practical SEO work for a trek operator:

- One strong page per trek, not three near-duplicates for 12, 14 and 16 days. Put the variations on one page.
- Trip reports written by guides, with dates, trail conditions and photos from that departure.
- Other languages only where you have native speakers to write the pages and answer enquiries, with hreflang set up as described in our [international SEO guide](/blog/international-seo-guide).
- Structured data for your organisation, trips and FAQs. Google does not show review stars for reviews a business hosts about itself, so put your effort into genuine reviews on Google and independent platforms.

## Why do speed and photos matter so much?

Trekking sites are photo-heavy, and travellers browse on phones. A trip page with 60 full-size photos can weigh tens of megabytes and crawl on a hotel Wi-Fi connection or a mobile network abroad.

Resize images to the size they display at, serve modern formats such as WebP or AVIF, lazy-load galleries below the fold, and put the site behind a CDN so pages load from servers near the visitor. Test from Europe and North America, not only from Kathmandu. Aim for the Core Web Vitals "good" thresholds: LCP within 2.5 seconds, INP within 200 milliseconds and CLS within 0.1.

## Should you rely on OTAs and tour marketplaces?

Use them for reach, not as the whole business. Marketplaces such as TourRadar, Bookmundi, GetYourGuide and Viator bring travellers who would never have found you, but they take a commission and keep the customer relationship. Your own website is where referrals, returning trekkers and people who found you on Google book directly. ## Frequently asked questions

### How much does a trekking agency website cost in Nepal?

As a typical market range, a well-built trekking agency website in Nepal costs roughly NPR 1 to 5 lakh, depending on the number of trips, languages, custom design, and whether it includes fixed-departure booking and online deposits. Content matters as much as code, so budget time for accurate itineraries. Our guide to [website costs in Nepal](/blog/website-cost-in-nepal) explains what moves the price.

### Should a trekking company show prices on its website?

Yes. Travellers compare prices before they enquire, and a page without one tends to lose them to a page that has it. Show a clear per-person price by group size, what it includes and excludes, and the currency. If prices vary by season or route option, give a "from" price and explain what changes it.

### Does a trekking website need to be in several languages?

Only where you can support the language. A German page attracts German-speaking travellers who will write in German and expect replies in German. Start with excellent English, add languages where you already get bookings and have staff or guides who speak them, and have native speakers write or check every page.

### Is WordPress a good choice for a trekking agency website?

Yes, for most agencies, if it is built carefully: a custom trip content type with structured fields for altitude, days and price, a light theme, few plugins, and good hosting with a CDN. Very large operators with complex departures and pricing may justify a custom system. Avoid heavy page builders, which make photo-rich trip pages slow.
