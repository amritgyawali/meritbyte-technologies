A website project brief is a short document, usually two to five pages, that tells a developer what your site is for, who it serves, what it must do, what content exists, and your budget, deadline and constraints. It is written by you, in plain language, before you ask for quotes.

A clear brief lets several firms quote the same project, cuts days out of discovery and surfaces misunderstandings while they are still cheap. The template below can be copied section by section; fill in what you know and write "unknown" for the rest.

## What is a website project brief, and how is it different from a requirements document?

A brief explains the why and the what from the business side. A website requirements document, or scope of work, is the detailed specification of how it will be built, usually written by the developer after discovery and agreed by both sides.

A web design brief is a narrower version focused on look, brand and user experience. For most small and mid-size projects, one good project brief covers the design side too, and the developer turns it into the requirements document.

## What should a website brief include?

A complete brief answers twelve questions, which match the template sections below:

1. What is the project and who decides?
2. What should it achieve, and how will you measure that?
3. Who is it for?
4. What exists today that should be kept, fixed or removed?
5. Which pages does it need?
6. Which features, and how important is each?
7. Who provides the content?
8. How should it look and feel?
9. What must it connect to, and what rules apply?
10. What are the budget and timeline?
11. What happens after launch?
12. What do you want back from the firms you send it to?

## How much detail is enough?

Enough that two firms reading it would quote roughly the same project. For a small business site that is often two pages; for a store or web application, closer to five. Longer is not better if the extra length is feature wish-lists rather than decisions.

Describe problems rather than prescribing solutions. "Customers keep phoning to ask whether we have stock" leads to a better answer than "we need a chatbot", because the developer can suggest a simpler fix, such as showing stock levels on product pages.

## Common mistakes in a web design brief

- **No budget.** Without a range, firms guess, and you get quotes for three different projects.
- **Everything marked "must".** If every feature is essential, nothing can be trimmed when the quote comes back high.
- **Vague style words.** "Modern and clean" describes most websites; name real examples instead.
- **Forgetting content.** Late content is one of the most frequent reasons projects stall; say who is writing it.
- **Hiding the awkward parts.** If a former developer holds the domain, or a system has no API, say so upfront.
- **Too many approvers.** List who signs off. Unexpected approvers late in the project cause rework.

## What happens after you send the brief?

Send the same brief to two to four firms and give them a week to ask questions. Share every answer with all of them so the quotes stay comparable. The questions a firm asks tell you a lot about how it would run the project.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a website brief be?

Usually two to five pages. A small business site can be briefed in two pages if the goals, pages, features, content responsibilities, budget and deadline are clear. Online stores and web applications need more detail on features, integrations and data. The test is whether two different firms reading it would quote roughly the same project, not the page count.

### Should I include my budget in a website brief?

Yes. A budget range lets firms recommend the right approach for your money, such as a well-built theme instead of a fully custom design, or a phased launch. Without it, you receive quotes for very different projects that cannot be compared. Giving a range does not mean firms will simply quote the maximum; good ones will explain what fits within it.

### What is the difference between a brief and a scope of work?

The brief is written by you and explains goals, audience, needs and constraints in business language. The scope of work, or requirements document, is written by the developer after discovery and specifies exactly what will be built, what is excluded, and how success is tested. The brief is the starting point for the scope, and both should be kept together.

### Do I need a brief for a small website?

Yes, even a one-page one. A short brief covering goals, pages, who writes the content, budget and deadline prevents the most common problems on small projects: mismatched expectations, stalled content and quotes that cannot be compared. It takes an hour or two to write and usually saves far more time than that during the build.
