White label web development is an arrangement where a partner firm builds websites that your agency sells, delivers and supports under your own brand. You keep the client, the strategy, the design direction and the invoice; the partner writes the code, tests it and often maintains it, without appearing anywhere your client sees.

Done well, it lets a design or marketing agency take on development work it could not staff for. Done badly, it puts your reputation in the hands of people you have never met. The difference is almost entirely in what gets agreed before the first project.

## What is white-label web development?

It is outsourced production sold under the agency's name. The partner is a subcontractor, and the end client's contract, and relationship, stays with the agency.

There are a few common shapes:

- **Project-based.** The agency sends a designed and scoped project; the partner quotes, builds and hands back a finished site.
- **Dedicated capacity.** One or more developers work on the agency's projects for a set number of days each month.
- **Embedded.** Partner engineers join the agency's standups, tools and ticket board as if they were in-house.
- **Maintenance only.** The partner takes over updates, fixes and small changes across the agency's existing client sites.

"White label" usually means the partner is invisible to the client. Some agencies prefer a transparent model where they tell clients a specialist partner handles development. Both are legitimate; decide which you are offering and make sure your client contracts reflect it.

## Why agencies use a white label agency partner

The usual reasons are capacity, skills and time. Margin comes up too, but agencies that choose a partner on price alone tend to regret it.

- **Capacity peaks.** Three projects land in the same month, and hiring permanent developers for a peak is expensive.
- **Skills you do not have.** Headless builds with Next.js, complex WooCommerce stores, custom WordPress blocks, API integrations.
- **Overnight progress.** A partner several hours ahead can move work forward while your team sleeps.
- **Focus.** Your team stays on strategy, design and client relationships.

The trade-offs are real. You have less direct control, more coordination work, and a quality problem at the partner becomes your problem with the client. White-label work is not a fit if your projects change daily in response to live client meetings, or if you have no one in-house who can review code or at least review builds carefully.

## How the partnership works, step by step

A reliable white-label workflow looks similar across most good partnerships:

1. **Pilot project.** Start with something small and real: a landing page, a set of WordPress templates, a plugin fix. Judge the communication as much as the code.
2. **Handoff package.** For each project the agency supplies approved designs (usually in Figma), final or near-final copy, a sitemap, a list of integrations and acceptance criteria.
3. **Estimate and scope.** The partner returns a written scope, timeline and price with exclusions. The agency adds its margin and quotes the client.
4. **Build on staging.** Work happens on an unbranded staging URL, ideally on a subdomain you control, so you can show clients progress without revealing the partner.
5. **Agency review.** Your team checks the build against the designs and acceptance criteria before the client sees it.
6. **Client feedback.** The agency collects and consolidates feedback, then passes one list to the partner rather than forwarding every email.
7. **Launch and handover.** Deployment, redirects, analytics checks and documentation. Repositories and hosting stay under your account or your client's.
8. **Support.** Either the partner handles post-launch fixes and maintenance under your brand, or your team takes over with the documentation.

## Pricing models for outsourced web development for agencies

Partners price in a few standard ways. Each suits a different kind of agency.

| Model | How it is billed | Best for | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed price per project | A quote per scoped project | Agencies selling defined website packages | Change requests eating the margin you built in |
| Time and materials | Hourly or daily rate for hours logged | Unpredictable or ongoing work | Budgets drifting without weekly visibility |
| Dedicated capacity | A set number of developer days per month | Agencies with steady volume | Paying for idle days in quiet months |
| Per template or per page | A rate per unique page template | WordPress builds from finished designs | Complex templates priced like simple ones |

Whatever the model, ask for weekly reporting in a format you could forward to a client without editing.

## White-label WordPress development: what to specify

Most white-label work is WordPress, and most WordPress problems come from undecided standards. Write these down once and attach them to every project:

- **Build approach.** Native blocks, custom blocks, Advanced Custom Fields, or a page builder such as Elementor. Mixing approaches across projects makes maintenance painful.
- **Plugin policy.** An approved list, a rule for adding new ones, and whose account holds premium licences. Licences should belong to your agency or the client, never the partner.
- **Coding standards.** WordPress Coding Standards checked with PHPCS, code in Git, no editing files on the live server.
- **Hosting and deployment.** Which hosts you use, how staging works, and how changes reach production.
- **Accessibility.** WCAG 2.2 level AA as the target, with keyboard and screen-reader checks rather than an overlay widget.
- **Performance.** Core Web Vitals targets and an image policy, checked on staging before handover.
- **Handover.** A short document per site: plugins and why, custom code locations, credentials stored in your password manager.

## Contracts, confidentiality and code ownership

The contract protects the thing you are actually selling: your client relationship. At minimum it should cover:

- **Confidentiality.** An NDA covering client identities, data and your pricing.
- **Non-solicitation.** The partner will not approach or accept work directly from your clients for a defined period.
- **IP assignment.** Code and designs transfer to you, or your client, on payment.
- **Accounts.** Repositories, hosting and third-party services are created under your organization or your client's, with the partner invited as a user.
- **Data protection.** If the partner can see personal data, such as form submissions or customer orders, it is acting as a sub-processor. Under GDPR and UK GDPR, a processor needs the controller's written authorization to use one, so check your client contracts before you hand anything over.
- **Exit.** What happens to work in progress, access and documentation if either side ends the arrangement.

## Making the time-zone gap work for you

Time zones help when handoffs are planned and hurt when they are not. Nepal, for example, is UTC+5:45 with no daylight saving: 9 hours 45 minutes ahead of US Eastern time in summer and 10 hours 45 minutes in winter, 4 hours 45 minutes ahead of the UK in summer, and 4 hours 15 minutes behind Sydney outside daylight saving.

For a US agency that means a brief sent at the end of your day can come back with progress on staging by your next morning, with a short overlap in the Nepal evening for live questions. ## Red flags in a white-label partner

- Reluctance to start with a small pilot.
- No staging environment, or staging you cannot open whenever you like.
- Domains, repositories or licences set up in the partner's name.
- No clear answer about who is actually writing the code, or signs of quiet sub-subcontracting.
- No written process for bugs found after launch.
- An accessibility answer that amounts to "we install a plugin".

## Frequently asked questions

### Is white-label web development ethical?

Yes, when your contracts allow it and you stay accountable for the result. Subcontracting is normal across agencies, architecture and law. The line is misrepresentation: if a client contract forbids subcontractors or requires named staff, you need permission first. Many agencies simply tell clients they work with a specialist development partner while keeping all communication and accountability themselves.

### Who owns the code in a white-label arrangement?

The contract decides, so write it down. The usual arrangement is that intellectual property transfers to the agency, or directly to the end client, on payment, and that repositories, hosting and licences are created under the agency's or client's accounts from day one. That way nothing breaks, and nothing needs to be handed back, if the partnership ends.

### How do I test a white-label partner without risking a client?

Give them a small, real, low-stakes job first: an internal landing page, a template for an existing site or a maintenance backlog. Judge how they ask questions, how clear their weekly updates are, whether estimates hold, and how they handle a change mid-task. Review the code or ask someone to. Only then hand over a project a key client depends on.

### Can a white-label partner talk to my clients directly?

It can, but decide the rule before the first project. Most agencies keep all client contact themselves and pass consolidated feedback to the partner. Some let the partner join technical calls under the agency's brand. If the partner does speak to clients, agree how they introduce themselves so nobody is put in an awkward or misleading position.
