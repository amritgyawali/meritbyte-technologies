B2B content marketing works for IT and professional services firms when it does one job: answering, in public and in detail, the questions a buyer would otherwise have to ask a salesperson. What will this cost, how long will it take, what can go wrong, how do you compare with the alternatives, and have you done this before? Firms that publish honest answers to those questions get shortlisted; firms that publish "10 benefits of cloud computing" mostly do not.

This guide is for software companies, agencies, IT consultancies, accountants and law firms whose sales cycle runs to weeks or months and involves more than one decision-maker.

## Why do most IT company blogs produce no leads?

Because they answer questions that nobody about to buy is asking, in a voice that sounds like every competitor. The traffic they attract is students and junior engineers, not people with a budget.

The usual pattern: a marketing coordinator or freelance writer receives a keyword list, produces two posts a month on topics like "What is DevOps?", and nobody from delivery ever reads them. Three failures repeat:

- **No point of view.** The article could carry any competitor's logo without changing a word.
- **No evidence of doing the work.** No numbers from projects, no trade-offs, no "we tried this and it failed because".
- **No path to a conversation.** Nothing connects the reader to a service page, a pricing explanation or a next step.

## What should an IT or professional services firm write about?

Start with the questions your sales team hears on every call, then the ones buyers are too cautious to ask. Marcus Sheridan's *They Ask, You Answer* framed the core set well: cost, problems, comparisons, reviews and "best of" lists. For a technical services firm, that translates into content like this:

| Buyer question | Content that answers it | Example for a software firm |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What will this cost? | Pricing guide with typical ranges and cost drivers | An explainer on [how custom software projects are priced](/blog/custom-software-development-cost) |
| Which option should we choose? | Honest comparison, including when off-the-shelf wins | Build vs buy for a customer portal |
| What could go wrong? | Risk and failure write-ups | Why data migrations overrun and how to scope them |
| How does working with you run? | Process page, sample weekly update, sample statement of work | A redacted status report |
| Have you done this for a business like ours? | Industry and integration pages | Connecting WooCommerce orders to Xero |
| Is it safe to hand this over? | Ownership, security and handover documentation | Who owns the code and the cloud account after launch |

Notice what is missing: definitions. "What is Kubernetes?" already has good answers from the vendor's own documentation. A buyer who needs that definition is not yet choosing a supplier, and your version adds nothing.

## How is thought leadership content different from SEO content?

SEO content answers a question someone is already searching for; thought leadership content argues for a position the reader has not yet considered. A services firm needs both, and the strongest pieces do both at once.

Thought leadership fails when it is opinion without evidence. It works when a named practitioner makes a specific, arguable claim backed by work they have done: "We stopped estimating fixed-price work in story points, and this is what replaced it." That kind of piece is hard to fake, which is exactly why it builds trust with a technical buyer.

Google's guidance on helpful, people-first content, in the [Google Search Central documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs), asks whether content shows first-hand experience and expertise, and its quality rater guidelines describe this as E-E-A-T: experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust. You do not need to game it. Put the author's real name and role on the piece, and include the details only someone who did the work would know.

AI writing tools are fine for outlines and editing. But a post assembled from what a language model already knows adds nothing a buyer cannot get by asking the model directly. The value is in the part only your team knows.

## How do you get engineers and consultants to contribute?

Do not ask them to write. Interview them for 30 minutes, record the call, and let an editor turn the transcript into a draft they only have to check.

1. Keep a running list of questions from sales calls, proposals, support tickets and onboarding. That list is your editorial calendar.
2. Each week, pick one question and book 30 minutes with the person who answers it best.
3. Push for specifics: the last time this came up, what it cost, what went wrong, what they would tell a friend.
4. The editor drafts within two days, keeping the expert's phrasing and examples.
5. The expert reviews for accuracy only, not style. Fifteen minutes.
6. Publish under the expert's name.

Existing material is often half-written already: estimate templates, architecture decision records, sanitized post-mortems, answers to security questionnaires. Get the client's permission before using anything that identifies them.

## How do you pick B2B SEO content topics with buying intent?

Favor queries that contain a buying signal, such as cost, pricing, "vs", alternative, migration, company or agency, even when keyword tools show tiny search volumes.

In B2B, one enquiry can be worth more than a year of traffic from a popular definition. Tools often show zero volume for phrases like "migrate Access database to web app", but those figures are estimates, and the few people typing that phrase are usually trying to hire someone. Patterns worth mining:

- `[service] cost` and `[service] pricing`
- `[option A] vs [option B]` and `[product] alternative`
- `[service] for [industry]`
- `how to choose a [provider type]`
- `[old system] to [new system] migration`
- the problem described in the buyer's words, not the engineer's

Search is no longer the only place buyers ask. AI assistants and answer engines quote pages that state answers plainly and consistently, which is covered in our guides to [answer engine optimization](/blog/answer-engine-optimization-aeo) and [generative engine optimization](/blog/generative-engine-optimization-geo). If you sell across borders, write in the buyer's terms: a firm in South Asia writing for American clients should use US spelling, dollars and US legal references, and should understand [what US buyers expect from an offshore partner](/website-developer/usa).

## Where should B2B content be distributed?

Wherever your buyers already spend attention. For most IT and professional services firms that means your own sales conversations, LinkedIn and an email list.

- **Sales follow-up.** The most underused channel. When a prospect asks a hard question, reply with the article; it saves time and shows depth.
- **LinkedIn, from people.** The author posting the key argument in the post itself, not just a link, usually starts more conversations than the company page sharing the same URL.
- **Email.** A monthly note to past clients, prospects and referrers, sent with proper consent. Our guide to [email marketing for small businesses](/blog/email-marketing-for-small-business) covers the setup.
- **Proposals.** Link relevant pieces so members of the buying committee who missed the call can catch up.
- **Communities.** Answer questions in forums and industry groups, linking only when the article genuinely answers the question.

## How do you measure B2B content marketing?

By pipeline: the enquiries, meetings and deals that content started or influenced. Traffic and rankings are early signals, not results.

| Measure | What it tells you | Where to get it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enquiries that mention an article | Direct influence | A free-text "How did you hear about us?" field on the contact form |
| Pages viewed before an enquiry | Which content assists | GA4 path exploration or CRM page tracking |
| Impressions and clicks for buying-intent queries | Whether you appear when buyers search | Google Search Console |
| Citations in AI answers | Visibility in assistants | Regular manual checks of your core questions in ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity and Google AI Overviews |
| Win rate on deals where content was shared | Whether content helps close | CRM notes from sales |

Plan on at least two quarters before judging the program. Pages need time to be indexed and to earn rankings, and B2B buyers often read for weeks before they get in touch.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should a B2B IT company publish content?

Substance matters more than frequency. One well-researched piece every week or two, answering a real buyer question with specifics from your own projects, will usually do more than daily short posts. Pick a pace your experts can sustain for a year, because a burst of posts followed by months of silence tells readers nobody is minding the site.

### Can we use AI to write our B2B content?

AI tools help with outlining, transcribing expert interviews, tightening drafts and spotting gaps. They are weak at the part that makes B2B content valuable: first-hand detail, figures from real projects and opinions your team will defend in a sales meeting. Use AI to speed up editing, and make sure a named practitioner supplies and checks the substance.

### Should we gate whitepapers and guides behind a form?

Gate only material that is worth an email address, such as a detailed template or a benchmark built from your own data. Keep explanatory content open: gated pages cannot rank in search or be quoted by AI assistants, and buyers researching quietly will simply read a competitor's open version instead of filling in your form.

### How long does B2B content marketing take to produce leads?

Expect months rather than weeks, and plan on two quarters before deciding whether it works. Early signs are Search Console impressions for buying-intent queries and sales hearing prospects mention your articles. Enquiries that name a specific piece usually follow, provided each article links clearly to the relevant service and a way to get in touch.
