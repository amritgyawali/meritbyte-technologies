A website for a law firm or accounting firm has three jobs: prove competence, stay inside professional advertising rules and handle confidential documents safely. Firms that get it right publish specific practice pages written or reviewed by qualified people, make booking a consultation simple, and move document exchange out of email into a secure client portal. A consulting firm website follows the same pattern with fewer regulatory limits.

## What do clients check before they contact a firm?

Credentials, relevance and signs of life. Someone with a tax dispute or a contract problem wants to see that you handle their exact issue in their jurisdiction, and that a named, qualified person will deal with it.

That translates into specific pages:

- One page per practice area or service, naming the jurisdictions and client types you handle.
- A profile for each lawyer, accountant or consultant with admissions, licenses or memberships and the matters they handle.
- Fees or fee structures where you can publish them, since many clients want a starting point before calling.
- How the first consultation works: free or paid, length, in person or by video, and expected response time.

## How do you write authority content without breaking the rules?

Write from real client questions, credit a qualified author, and date everything. Legal and financial topics count as "Your Money or Your Life" content in Google's quality rater guidelines, so each page needs a named author with credentials, a review date, a note on which jurisdiction it covers, and a statement that it is general information rather than advice.

Answer the questions clients actually ask ("Do I need to register for VAT?", "What happens at a first hearing?") in plain language, and revise pages when the law changes. ## Professional advertising rules by market

Every regulator below limits what firms may claim. Treat the table as a starting map, not legal advice, and read your regulator's current text.

| Market | Who sets the rules | Typical website implications |
| --- | --- | --- |
| USA | State bars, many following ABA Model Rules 7.1-7.3; state boards of accountancy | No misleading claims; some states require "Attorney Advertising" labels or results disclaimers; only licensees may use "CPA" |
| UK | Solicitors Regulation Authority; ICAEW and ACCA for accountants | SRA Transparency Rules: price information for certain services, complaints process, the SRA digital badge |
| Canada | Provincial law societies; provincial CPA bodies | Marketing rules in each code of conduct; limits on calling yourself a specialist |
| Australia | State regulators under the Legal Profession Uniform Law or state acts; Tax Practitioners Board for tax agents | Restrictions on personal injury advertising in some states, such as New South Wales and Queensland |
| Nepal | Nepal Bar Council; Institute of Chartered Accountants of Nepal (ICAN) | Codes of conduct that limit how members promote themselves |

Superlatives such as "best tax lawyer in town" and unqualified success rates are risky almost everywhere.

## Client portals and secure document exchange

Tax returns, contracts and passport scans should not travel as email attachments. A client portal gives each client a login, a document area and a message thread, with access logs and encryption at rest.

Build or buy? Practice software often includes a portal: Clio for law firms, TaxDome or SmartVault for accounting firms, or SharePoint for firms already on Microsoft 365. A custom portal pays off when you need a workflow those tools cannot handle, such as a guided intake that feeds your own case system, or identity checks for UK client due diligence under the Money Laundering Regulations 2017. Either way, require multi-factor authentication and set retention periods. ## Booking consultations without conflict problems

Collect enough to run a conflict check and nothing more. Ask for the names of the parties and the type of matter, not the full story, and state clearly that submitting the form does not create a lawyer-client relationship. Route each booking to someone who clears conflicts before confirming it.

Calendly or Microsoft Bookings can handle the scheduling itself. For an accounting firm website, the same logic applies to engagement letters: confirm scope and independence before the client uploads anything sensitive.

## What should a website for a law firm or accounting firm build first?

1. Practice-area pages and people profiles, with credentials and jurisdictions.
2. Consultation booking with a conflict-safe intake form.
3. A compliance pass against your regulator's rules, including disclaimers, required badges and price information.
4. Google Business Profile and local SEO for each office, as set out in our [Google Business Profile guide](/blog/local-seo-google-business-profile).
5. The client portal, once the front door works.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a law firm website show client reviews?

It depends on your regulator. Many US states allow testimonials with conditions, such as disclaimers about results, while other jurisdictions are stricter. Where reviews are allowed, collect them on Google or an independent platform rather than curating quotes, and never offer incentives in exchange for them. Check your bar or law society's current rules before publishing any.

### Is email safe for sending tax documents?

It is a poor channel for tax returns, IDs and bank statements. Attachments stay in inboxes indefinitely, get forwarded and are easily sent to the wrong address. Use a client portal or secure file-sharing tool with multi-factor authentication, and apply your firm's retention rules. Most clients adapt quickly once someone shows them how the portal works.

### How much does a law firm website cost?

A small firm site with practice pages, profiles and booking is a modest project. Adding a client portal, local SEO for several offices or multilingual content raises the cost considerably. Prices vary by market and provider, so compare quotes on identical scope and ask whether compliance review, content editing and ongoing security updates are included.

### Should a consulting firm website look like a law firm's?

No. A consulting firm website faces fewer advertising restrictions and has more room to show work: detailed project narratives with client permission, frameworks and opinionated articles. The trust signals shift too, toward named consultants, industries served and clear engagement models. The secure document handling and booking practices still apply.
